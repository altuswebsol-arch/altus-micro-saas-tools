## Intro

Search for anything on Google and every result has the same anatomy: a blue title, a green-ish web address, and two lines of grey text underneath. Those two lines are the meta description — and for most small business websites, they're an afterthought. The page got written, the title got written, and the description was left to chance.

That chance costs clicks. The meta description is your 155-character sales pitch, shown to every person who finds you on Google, before they've seen a single pixel of your website. Here's what it is, how to write one that works, and a free tool that checks yours in seconds.

## H2: What a meta description actually is

A meta description is a short summary of a page, written in the page's HTML code, that Google may show underneath the page title in search results. "May" is doing heavy lifting there — Google treats your description as a suggestion, not an order. If it likes yours, it shows it. If it doesn't, it writes its own from your page text.

It is not a ranking factor. Google has said so plainly, more than once. Stuffing keywords into it won't move you up the results. What it does is decide what happens after someone finds you: a clear, relevant description gets the click, and clicks are the entire point of ranking in the first place.

Think of it this way: the title wins the ranking, the description wins the click.

## H2: Where it shows up (and where it doesn't)

The meta description appears in Google's search results under your page title — the snippet. It also frequently becomes the preview text when someone shares your page on social media or in a message (though social platforms prefer their own Open Graph tags when those exist).

It does not appear on your page itself. Visitors never see it there. Its entire audience is people who haven't clicked yet — which is exactly why it matters more than most businesses realise.

## H2: How to write one that gets clicks — 5 rules

### H3: 1. Keep it 120–155 characters

Google shows roughly the first 155 characters — and it cuts by pixel width, not character count. A description full of wide letters (W, M, G) gets cut earlier than one full of narrow ones (i, l, t). The practical rule: aim for 120–155 characters, and put the important words in the first 120 so they survive even an aggressive cut.

### H3: 2. Put the keyword near the front

When the searcher's words appear in your description, Google bolds them — and bolded words draw the eye. Include the main keyword or phrase for the page, naturally, near the beginning. "Naturally" matters: "Web design Leeds | web design services | best web design" reads like spam and invites a rewrite.

### H3: 3. Say what the page gives, then say what to do

The best descriptions do two jobs in two beats: what this page is ("Affordable web design for Leeds small businesses — fixed prices, no jargon.") and what to do next ("Book a free consultation today."). That second beat — the call to action — is the most commonly missing ingredient. Tell the searcher the next step: book, shop, call, download, get a quote.

### H3: 4. Write it like a human, for a human

No all-caps. No exclamation-mark confetti. No copying the page title into the description field (surprisingly common — it wastes the space and reads terribly). One page, one unique description, in the same voice as the page itself.

### H3: 5. Every important page gets its own

Google rewrites descriptions it considers unhelpful, and "identical on forty pages" tops that list. You don't need to write one for every page on a 500-page site — but every page that matters for search (homepage, services, key landing pages) deserves its own unique description.

## H2: Why Google rewrites descriptions (and how to stop it)

Google rewrites your description when it thinks it can do better — typically when yours is missing, very short, stuffed with keywords, or duplicated across pages. It then assembles something from your page content, which may be accurate but is rarely persuasive.

You can't force Google to use yours. You can only make yours so clearly good — unique, relevant, the right length, matching the search intent — that Google has no reason to replace it. That's the whole game.

## H2: FAQs

### H3: Does the meta description help me rank higher?
Not directly — it's not a ranking factor. But a better description means a higher click-through rate, which means more visitors from the rankings you already have. For most small businesses, that's the cheaper win.

### H3: What happens if I don't write one?
Google writes one for you from your page text. Sometimes it's fine; often it's a random slice of a paragraph that says nothing persuasive. If any page matters to your business, write its description yourself.

### H3: Should each page have a different meta description?
Yes — at minimum, every page you want found on Google. Duplicate descriptions across pages are one of the main triggers for Google rewriting them.

### H3: Can a meta description be too long?
Yes. Anything past ~155 characters (or ~920 pixels wide) gets cut off mid-sentence in the results, which looks unfinished. Keep it tight and front-load the important words.
